Enclosure or barrier?

When a noise barrier is the better choice

An acoustic enclosure damps in all directions and achieves the highest values. A noise barrier screens only one side – but the unit stays open at the top, there is no pressure drop and it remains accessible from all sides.

How a barrier works

Interrupting the direct path

A noise barrier works as soon as it breaks the line of sight between the unit and the window. Sound then has to travel over the top edge – it is diffracted and loses energy. The longer this detour, the greater the screening.

  • Close to the source: A barrier close to the unit screens more than one halfway between unit and neighbour.
  • Height matters: The barrier must rise well above the sound source. A cantilever inclined 45° towards the unit increases the effect without building the wall higher.
  • Absorbing, not reflecting: HSA3 on the unit side stops the barrier from throwing sound to the other side or back onto the unit.
  • Extend beyond the ends: Otherwise sound simply travels around the ends of the barrier.

What a barrier can achieve

  • Line of sight just interrupted: around 5 dB
  • Typical for units in residential settings: 6–10 dB(A)
  • Theoretical limit to ISO 9613-2 for diffraction over one edge: 20 dB

Much more than 10 dB(A) requires tall barriers close to the unit – at that point an enclosure is usually the more economical solution.

Low frequencies

Why the hum still gets over the barrier

For outdoor sound propagation, the German TA Lärm refers to ISO 9613-2. A barrier works in three ways: it insulates the sound passing through it, it absorbs the sound that would otherwise be reflected back to the unit, and it forces the sound to detour over the top edge. How effective that detour is depends above all on frequency.

Proximity beats height – but only with absorption

Screening is described by the Fresnel number N = 2δ/λ: the ratio between the detour δ over the top edge and the wavelength λ. At low frequencies λ is large, the Fresnel number small and the screening weak. The most effective way to lengthen the detour is to place the barrier close to the source.

That is exactly where a new problem arises: sound bounces back and forth between the unit and the barrier. Every reflection acts like an additional source that also radiates over the top. At short distances, resonances also build up and amplify tonal noise.

A barrier close to the source therefore needs a highly absorbing source side – for low frequencies with tuned resonance absorbers instead of conventional mineral wool.

Worked example: absorption replaces height

  • Frequency 300 Hz, distance unit – barrier 1 m
  • Barrier with 20 % versus 90 % absorption: about 6.5 dB more at the receiver
  • Compensating by height alone: a barrier about 3.0 to 3.5 m higher
  • Limit for diffraction over one edge to ISO 9613-2: 20 dB

Beyond a certain height, missing absorption cannot be compensated at all. Good absorption is almost always cheaper than extra barrier height. Our own calculation example to ISO 9613-2; real values depend on geometry, source and receiver position.

From practice

The most common mistakes with noise barriers

One tonal peak devalues the barrier

AC units often run for hours at the same operating point, so individual peaks become audible as tones. TA Lärm adds a penalty of 3 or 6 dB for this; for low-frequency noise DIN 45680 also applies. One unabsorbed primary peak can cancel out the effect of an entire barrier.

Resonances at short distances

When the barrier stands close to the unit, resonances form if the frequency concerned is not sufficiently absorbed. Partly roofed enclosures with openings are especially critical. Permanently tonal noise is the most common cause of complaints from residents.

Air short-circuit and open flanks

Supply and exhaust air must not mix, otherwise efficiency drops and the unit runs louder. Every barrier therefore needs openings for fresh air – and sound escapes there. Only high absorption on the source side can compensate for this.

Ratings from the wrong sound field

Absorption coefficients from the reverberation room apply to a diffuse sound field. In the confined near field of a unit, many materials behave very differently. Planning with reverberation-room values alone often overestimates porous absorbers.

Our approach

Designed for the near field

Reverberation-room values to ISO 354 are the basis for comparison – we publish them for HSA3 too. What counts for the design, however, is how the barrier performs where it stands: close to the unit. In the near field and in partly enclosed spaces, porous absorbers lose effect because reflections increase sharply in the confined space and the material saturates. Numerous measurements of our own confirm this.

  • Metal resonance absorbers: HSA3 is tuned to the unit's primary peak, including the low-frequency range.
  • Weatherproof and durable: no soaked mineral wool, no fibres, no loss of performance over the years.
  • No heat build-up: metal absorbs no moisture and releases heat. Optionally, the surface can be used for adiabatic pre-cooling of the intake air.
  • Simulation-based design: acoustics, airflow (CFD) and BIM planning, so noise control and air routing fit from the start.

Frequently asked questions

How high does the noise barrier need to be?

High enough to reliably break the line of sight between the sound source and the relevant window – with a margin. If the window is on an upper floor, the barrier has to be correspondingly higher. The configurator suggests the barrier height for your situation.

Is a barrier enough for a neighbour on the second floor?

Often not. The higher the window, the less a barrier on the ground screens. In that case an enclosure with a tube silencer in the roof is usually the better solution.

Does the barrier restrict the unit's air flow?

No, as long as the distance to the unit is kept. The barrier is open at the top; we agree the distance based on the unit's air side so that no exhaust air is drawn back in.

Why absorbing and not just a solid wall?

A hard wall reflects sound – onto the unit, onto facades opposite or towards other neighbours. With HSA3 on the unit side, the sound is absorbed instead of redirected.

Why can the unit still be heard humming despite a noise barrier?

Low frequencies have wavelengths of several metres and bend over the top edge into the shadow zone. In addition, sound reflected back and forth between unit and barrier also radiates over the top. The remedy is a source side that absorbs precisely the low frequencies – not just a higher barrier.

What is the tonality penalty?

If a single tone stands out clearly from the noise, the German TA Lärm adds a penalty of 3 or 6 dB to the rating level, depending on how prominent it is. This is common with AC units and heat pumps in continuous operation. That is why we tune the absorption to the unit's primary peak.
